Free algebra reference

Function transformations

How a graph moves, flips, and stretches when you change its equation. Every example below starts from the parabola y = x2, shown as a dashed curve.

Start with the parent function

y = f(x)  ·  f(x) = x2

General form

y = a f(b(x − h)) + k

  • aVertical stretch or compression. A negative a flips over the x-axis.
  • bHorizontal stretch or compression, by a factor of 1/b. A negative b flips over the y-axis.
  • hHorizontal shift. x − h moves right; x + h moves left.
  • kVertical shift. + k moves up; − k moves down.

Transformations chart

Tap any card or box to see it larger.

  • f(x) + k Vertical shift Up k units y = x2 + 2
  • f(x) − k Vertical shift Down k units y = x2 − 2
  • f(x − h) Horizontal shift Right h units y = (x − 2)2
  • f(x + h) Horizontal shift Left h units y = (x + 2)2
  • −f(x) Reflection Flip over the x-axis y = −x2
  • f(−x) Reflection Flip over the y-axis y = (x − 2)2 becomes y = (−x − 2)2
  • a f(x), a > 1 Vertical stretch Taller and narrower y = 3x2
  • a f(x), 0 < a < 1 Vertical compression Shorter and wider y = 0.5x2
  • f(bx), b > 1 Horizontal compression Narrower, by a factor of 1/b y = (2x)2
  • f(bx), 0 < b < 1 Horizontal stretch Wider, by a factor of 1/b y = (0.5x)2

Why the y-axis example uses (x − 2)2: the plain parabola y = x2 is symmetric about the y-axis, so f(−x) leaves it unchanged. A shifted parabola shows the flip.

Four rules to memorize

  1. Outside is vertical, and it does what it says. f(x) + 3 moves up 3.
  2. Inside is horizontal, and it does the opposite. f(x − 3) moves right 3.
  3. A negative outside flips over the x-axis. A negative inside flips over the y-axis.
  4. Inside numbers work as reciprocals. f(2x) is half as wide; f((1/2)x) is twice as wide.

One-line cheat sheet

Outside = vertical, as written.   Inside = horizontal, opposite.   Negative outside = x-axis flip.   Negative inside = y-axis flip.   Inside stretches use the reciprocal.

From Socrates to Kuhn

Six evenings, six turning points. Each session pairs a short primary reading with a discussion of one question: how do we know what we claim to know?

  1. Athens, 5th–4th century BCE

    Socrates and Plato: knowledge through questions

    Socrates claimed to know only that he knew nothing. Plato built on his method and argued that true knowledge concerns unchanging forms, not shifting appearances.

    Reading: Plato, Apology and the allegory of the cave (Republic, Book VII)
  2. 4th century BCE to 13th century

    Aristotle to Aquinas: logic, observation, and faith

    Aristotle grounded knowledge in observation and formal logic. His work reached medieval Europe largely through Arabic translations and commentators such as Ibn Rushd, and Thomas Aquinas joined it to Christian theology.

    Reading: Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book I (excerpt); Aquinas, the five ways (excerpt)
  3. 1600s

    Bacon, Galileo, Descartes, Newton: the search for method

    The scientific revolution replaced appeals to authority with experiment and mathematics. Descartes tried to rebuild all knowledge from a single certainty.

    Reading: Descartes, Discourse on Method, Part II
  4. 1700s

    Hume and Kant: what experience can prove

    Hume showed that no number of observations proves a general law. Kant answered that the mind itself supplies the structure of experience.

    Reading: Hume, An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Section IV; Kant, "What Is Enlightenment?" (1784)
  5. 1800s

    Mill, Marx, Darwin, Nietzsche: liberty, power, and change

    Four thinkers turned the question toward society and history: free debate, economic power, natural selection, and the origin of values.

    Reading: Mill, On Liberty, Chapter 2; Marx and Engels, The Communist Manifesto, Part I
  6. 1934–1962

    Popper and Kuhn: how science changes

    Popper argued that a scientific claim must be testable and open to refutation. Kuhn argued that science moves through paradigms and occasional revolutions, not a steady pile-up of facts.

    Reading: Popper, "Science: Conjectures and Refutations" (excerpt); Kuhn, The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (1962), excerpt

This course follows one tradition. It is not the only tradition of rigorous thought, and it did not develop alone. Where Western thinkers drew on Islamic, Indian, Chinese, African, or Indigenous scholarship, the course says so.
